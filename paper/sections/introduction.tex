\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Users of MIP solvers in planning and scheduling often need a good feasible solution within a fixed time, not a proof
of optimality. The primal integral $P(T)$ measures how soon a solver gets there: it averages the gap to the best known
value over the time budget $T$~\citep{berthold2013primal}. Lower is better; $P = 1$ if no solution was found.

GPUs now sit beside CPU solvers on small machines, and they suit population search: many candidate solutions can be
repaired at once. NVIDIA cuOpt already runs GPU heuristics and a GPU population beside its own
branch-and-bound~\citep{corduk2025gpuprimal,nvidia2026cuoptsrc}. On the machine used here, running cuOpt beside SCIP
or HiGHS without exchange lowered the primal integral at 60\,s by 21\,\% and 30\,\%~\citep{orbay2026optopt}; there, a
CPU population exchanging solutions with SCIP gave no detectable gain.

I-TREA II moves the population to the GPU, as an evolutionary algorithm (EA), and couples it with exact solvers while
they run; during development it grew to three processes on one NVIDIA DGX Spark (Figure~\ref{fig:system}). From the
first I-TREA~\citep{orbay2011itrea} it keeps one principle: infeasible solutions whose objective is better than the
best feasible one keep a capped share of the population.

The aim is not to be better on average, but to help each solver where it is weakest, with the average as a guard.
This report covers phase~1: development on 17 public instances at 120\,s, up to a checkpoint that decides whether to
continue. Its question: does the coupled system lower the primal integral on instances hard for cuOpt alone or SCIP
alone, without being worse than cuOpt alone on average?

The report gives the method fixed after phase~1 (Section~\ref{sec:method}), what each tested change did and the
checkpoint result with its main loss (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and a benchmark harness that gives every compared GPU
solver the same cores and GPU (Appendix~\ref{app:harness}).
