\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
All numbers are descriptive, interim development data (17 instances, 120-s runs, 6--16 October).

\subsection{What phase~1 changed, and what helped}\label{sec:res-dev}
Phase~1 kept six of the ten changes it tested (Table~\ref{tab:changes}). The largest change was structural. On eight
new instances, the two-process coupling of SCIP and the EA had a higher $P$ than cuOpt alone on five and a lower one
on one (mean $d$ $-0.51$ $[-0.84, -0.12]$; test~3). So cuOpt joined as a third process instead of re-implementing
cuOpt's heuristics inside the EA (test~4).

The last two changes kept, LP decoding and the yielding wait, were tested apart and first ran together in the
checkpoint runs. LP decoding lowered $P$ on both of its target instances on all four seeds: \texttt{item\_placement}
from 0.330 to 0.198 and \texttt{sing44} from 0.473 to 0.411 (test~12). The yielding wait let the LP relaxation on the
EA's core finish about twice as early on \texttt{ca} (20.0 and 20.2\,s instead of 37.3 and 38.3\,s). It was adopted
only after a follow-up, chosen once its first test had failed (test~11), lowered the bar on EA generations from 0.921 to
0.90 of the control's (test~15); its record estimates that this adds 12--19 percentage points to the chance of adopting
a harmful change.

\subsection{The checkpoint result}\label{sec:res-c1}
On four seeds, the coupled system had a lower $P$ than cuOpt alone on 7 of the 11 instances of cuOpt's hard set and a
higher one on 3 (Wilcoxon $p = 0.027$); the hint and the guard both passed their bars (Table~\ref{tab:readings}). Over
all 17 instances, $P$ was lower on 8, higher on 8 and equal on 1. Figure~\ref{fig:pi} shows a typical instance.

The two halves agree: seeds 0--1 read 0.809 and $+0.050$, seeds 2--3 0.787 and $+0.034$. Seeds 2--3 ran only because
seeds 0--1 passed the 0.95 bar, so they alone are the check against that selection.

Against SCIP alone, most of the drop on SCIP's hard set (0.889 to 0.448) is what cuOpt alone also reaches on the same
cores and GPU (0.517); on these 15, the coupled system had a lower $P$ than cuOpt alone on 7, an equal one on 1 and a
higher one on 7.

cuOpt alone's crashes favour the result: it ended with a segmentation fault on 3 of the 4 seeds of
\texttt{comp21-2idx} (not on 13 October), and these runs count with $P$ up to the crash (its one complete seed scored
0.520; seed mean 0.692, coupled 0.577), so that instance's gain rests on them. Without them that instance is incomplete, and the fixed rules read the hint and
the guard only on complete sets; on the other 10 hard instances, $P$ is lower on 6 and higher on 3 ($p = 0.055$).

The harm check (Table~\ref{tab:terms}) flagged two instances. On \texttt{glass4} (its $d_i$ $-0.114$, band 0.057), a
later four-seed check in one window put three configurations level (mean $P$ 0.218--0.223), so by a rule fixed
beforehand the flag reads as day-to-day drift (test~18). Such drift can far exceed a band: \texttt{item\_placement}'s
$P$ was 0.364 and 0.218 on two days with the same EA. On \texttt{comp21-2idx}, $d_i$ was $-0.051$ against a band of
0.050, at the edge.

\subsection{Where the system loses}\label{sec:res-loss}
The largest loss is \texttt{chromaticindex1024-7} ($d$ $-0.847$ on four seeds), the largest negative term in the
guard. Without the EA, cuOpt finds its first solution after 6.5--12.2\,s; inside the coupled system, only after about 65\,s
(Table~\ref{tab:loss}). There the EA's first generation is a 48-s GPU repair of about 1{,}300 back-to-back kernel
launches, and neither SCIP's presence nor cuOpt's thread count explains the delay (test~13). Some ways of backing the EA
off the GPU brought cuOpt back early, others did not (Table~\ref{tab:loss}; tests 7, 9 and 14); why is not yet known.
